# NYU and Washington Square: a history dossier (Step 8, research only)

Raw material for Avi's story. Nothing here goes into the game by itself. This is a sourced timeline of significant, well-documented events tied to places in the study area, each with the physical traces that could plausibly survive to 2126.

**Status: Stop point 3 draft (11 Oct 2026).** The table of contents is the full planned scope. Six entries are written out in full as samples (marked **Sample** in the contents). Everything else in the contents is a heading still to research. Where a date or detail in the contents comes from my general knowledge rather than a source I have read, it is tagged **[K]** and will be checked before its entry is written.

## Rules for this file

- Documented facts only, each with a source link. No invented quotes, actions or motives for real people.
- No private individuals named. Public officials, public figures acting in public and organisations can be named. People who were private citizens at the time (students, workers, victims, bystanders) are described by role, never by name, with one exception: the 146 Triangle fire victims, whose names are already published on the memorial, are referred to only through it and never listed here.
- **Contested** events are marked as such, with the main positions summarised fairly and attributed.
- Events involving deaths or tragedy are marked **Tragedy** and written plainly, without dramatising.
- **Brown Building and Triangle Fire memorial rule:** kept intact and dignified in 2126 while everything around them decays; never a subject for jokes, banners or satire. Their "traces" below are about how the building and memorial endure, not about decay.
- **Bobst atrium rule:** the atrium and its screens are architecture only. The dossier records that they exist and when, and nothing more.
- Places are linked to `RESEARCH/campus-inventory.md` by section number (for example "inventory 3.3").

## Entry format

Each entry has: **When**, **Where** (place and inventory link), **What happened**, **Why it mattered**, **Flags** (Contested, Tragedy, or none), **Traces in 2126** (plausible physical remains, as story material), **Sources**.

## Sample entries

### 4. The Stonecutters' Riot

- **When:** The mid-1830s. Most accounts give 1834; NYU's own Stonecutters Memorial marker gives 1833. Treat the year as **uncertain** (1833 or 1834) until a contemporary newspaper settles it.
- **Where:** Washington Square, where NYU's first building, the University Building, was going up on the east side of the square (the site of today's Silver Center, inventory 3.3). The memorial is in Schwartz Plaza.
- **What happened:** The contractors building the University Building bought dressed marble cut by prisoners at Sing Sing, which was much cheaper than hiring the city's stonecutters. The stonecutters held meetings, then marched through the streets with placards denouncing the contractors, and some attacked the houses of citizens. The 27th Regiment was called out and dispersed them. Fearing an attack on the unfinished building at night, part of the regiment camped in Washington Square in full view of it for four days and nights, until things calmed down and the work went on. NYU's marker says local stonecutters and marble workers had first petitioned against the plan without success, and that the unrest took three days to suppress.
- **Why it mattered:** One of the earliest labour disputes in New York over convict labour undercutting free workers, and the university's founding building was at the centre of it.
- **Flags:** None. (Date uncertain, as above.)
- **Traces in 2126:** The present-day Stonecutters Memorial is made of mycelium, a fungal material, so by 2126 it would be long gone or overgrown: a bare patch or a plaque mount in Schwartz Plaza. The Founders Memorial nearby holds a surviving piece of the University Building, which would outlast almost everything around it. Militia tents on the square are a period image that could return as an echo (a "leak" of the past).
- **Sources:**
  - J. T. Headley, *The Great Riots of New York, 1712 to 1873* (1873), chapter VI, "Stonecutters' Riot" (public domain): https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6856
  - Stonecutters Memorial marker, Schwartz Plaza (NYU Grounds and NYU Steinhardt), on HMdb: https://hmdb.org/m.asp?m=144204
  - Washington Square Park Conservancy, park history (gives 1834): https://www.washingtonsquarenyc.org/park-history
  - Campus of New York University (Sing Sing marble as the catalyst): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campus_of_New_York_University

### 8. The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire

- **When:** 25 March 1911. Later markers: ILGWU plaque 1961 (fiftieth anniversary); National Historic Landmark 1991; New York City landmark 25 March 2003; the memorial dedicated 11 October 2023.
- **Where:** The Asch Building, 23 to 29 Washington Place at Greene Street, built 1900 to 1901; now NYU's Brown Building (inventory 3.6). The Triangle Waist Company occupied the top three floors.
- **What happened:** A fire broke out on the eighth floor and spread upward. Locked doors and inadequate fire escapes contributed to the deaths of 146 workers, many of them young immigrant women. NYU leased the eighth floor in 1916, later occupied the whole building, and was given it in 1929, when it was renamed the Frederick Brown Building after the realtor who acquired it for the university.
- **Why it mattered:** The fire became a turning point for workplace safety and labour law in New York and the United States, and a lasting rallying point for the labour movement. The building is one of the most important sites in American labour history.
- **Flags:** **Tragedy.** Brown Building and memorial rule applies in full.
- **Traces in 2126:** Under the project rule, the building and memorial stay intact and dignified while the rest of the street decays. What exists today and would plausibly still be there: two bronze plaques on the easternmost pier of the Washington Place front (one from the National Park Service, one from the ILGWU); a bronze tablet in the lobby marking the 1929 dedication; and the 2023 memorial, a textured stainless steel ribbon about twelve feet above the sidewalk on the south and east fronts, with the victims' names and ages cut into it and mirrored in a dark reflective panel at hip height, survivor and eyewitness testimony etched along the panel's lower edge, and the story told in English, Yiddish and Italian. Stainless steel weathers slowly, so in 2126 it can still read clearly. A story could have it kept clean when nothing else is, which says something about who is still here.
- **Sources:**
  - NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Brown Building designation report, LP-2128 (2003): https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/2128.pdf
  - Remember the Triangle Fire Coalition, the memorial: https://rememberthetrianglefire.org/memorial/
  - Background: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_Shirtwaist_Factory_fire

### 10. Taking traffic out of the park

- **When:** 1935 to 1963. Decisive steps: 23 October 1958 (temporary closing authorised) and April 1959 (closed to all but emergency vehicles); the legal process completed in 1963.
- **Where:** Washington Square Park as a whole, especially the line of Fifth Avenue running south under the Arch (inventory 2.1 and 2.2).
- **What happened:** Fifth Avenue traffic, including buses, ran through the park. Robert Moses, as Parks Commissioner, proposed rerouting and road schemes in 1935, again in 1939, in 1947 (the "Rogers Plan", with a bus turn-out in the middle of the park) and in 1952, when he announced two 38-foot roads flanking the Arch and running through the park. Local residents led by Shirley Hayes and Edith Lyons formed the Washington Square Park Committee, gathered 4,000 signatures and changed the question from how wide the road should be to whether there should be one at all; the Board of Estimate shelved the 1952 plan. In 1958 Jane Jacobs, Eleanor Roosevelt, Margaret Mead, Lewis Mumford and others formed the Joint Emergency Committee to Close Washington Square Park to Traffic, which ran a petition and a rally in the park in May 1958. The Board of Estimate authorised a temporary closing on 23 October 1958, closed the park to all but emergency vehicles in April 1959, and completed the permanent closing in 1963.
- **Why it mattered:** A celebrated early defeat for Moses and a founding moment of neighbourhood activism in New York. It shaped the park as it is today, a pedestrian square with the Arch at its head, and its tactics fed later fights, including the ones with NYU.
- **Flags:** None (the outcome was contested at the time, but the facts are not).
- **Traces in 2126:** Under the paths, the ghost of the old roadbed through the Arch: a different paving or a line of old asphalt where tree roots heave the stone. A faded bus stop sign or kerb line could survive at the park edge. Petitions and flyers are paper and would not last, which is a story detail in itself.
- **Sources:**
  - New York Preservation Archive Project, "Washington Square": https://www.nypap.org/preservation-history/washington-square/
  - New York Preservation Archive Project, "Shirley Hayes": https://www.nypap.org/preservation-history/shirley-hayes/
  - NYC Parks, Washington Square Park history: https://php1-84.nycgovparks.org/parks/washington-square-park/highlights/9763

### 18. NYU 2031, the Core Plan, and Glick v. Harvey

- **When:** Announced 2007; approved by the City Council in 2012; litigated 2012 to 2015 (Supreme Court ruling 7 January 2014, Appellate Division 14 October 2014, Court of Appeals 30 June 2015). The flagship building, the John A. Paulson Center, opened in 2022 to 2023.
- **Where:** The two "superblocks" south of the square bounded by West 3rd Street, Houston Street, Mercer Street and LaGuardia Place (inventory 3.11 Silver Towers and Washington Square Village; 3.4 Paulson Center), and four strips of open space along their edges: Mercer Playground, LaGuardia Park, LaGuardia Corner Gardens and the Mercer-Houston Dog Run.
- **What happened:** NYU's plan, first called NYU 2031 and later the Core Plan, called for about six million square feet of new space, with about two million at Washington Square, much of it on the superblocks. It met wide opposition from residents and from NYU faculty and staff, including a group called NYU Faculty Against the Sexton Plan. After the City Council approved it in 2012, Assemblymember Deborah Glick, the Greenwich Village Society for Historic Preservation, the Historic Districts Council and other groups sued, arguing that the four strips had become parkland and could not be handed over without the State Legislature's approval (the public trust doctrine). In January 2014 Justice Donna Mills agreed in part and blocked building on those parcels without the Legislature's consent. The Appellate Division reversed in October 2014, and on 30 June 2015 the Court of Appeals ruled 7 to 0 for the City and NYU: the City's permits and licences had made Parks Department management of the strips "temporary and provisional", so there was no implied dedication as parkland, and public belief that they were permanent parks "does not warrant a contrary result".
- **Why it mattered:** The largest expansion fight in NYU's history, and a precedent on when long-used city land becomes legally protected parkland, which commentators argued weakens protection for community gardens across the city. It produced the Paulson Center (735,000 square feet: classrooms, a theatre, athletics, faculty apartments and a 400-student residence hall), which replaced the Coles Sports Center.
- **Flags:** **Contested.** Opponents (GVSHP, the Historic Districts Council, Assemblymember Glick, many faculty) said the plan was too large for the Village and that it took community parks; after the ruling GVSHP said it would have a devastating impact and weakened legal protection for parks. NYU and the City said the university needed the space, that the plan went through the full public approval process, and that the parcels were never legally parkland; the courts agreed on the legal point.
- **Traces in 2126:** Construction-era leftovers make good set dressing: a faded "community garden" sign on a chain-link fence along LaGuardia Place, a dog-run gate with no run behind it, a protest sticker or stencil on a lamp post. The Paulson Center's faceted glass towers are the newest large building in the area, so in 2126 they are the most striking ruin: blue glass cracked or missing panel by panel, its green roofs (25,000 square feet) gone wild.
- **Sources:**
  - Matter of Glick v Harvey, 25 NY3d 1175, 2015 NY Slip Op 05593 (Court of Appeals, 30 June 2015): https://www.nycourts.gov/REPORTER/3dseries/2015/2015_05593.htm
  - CityLand, "New York State Court of Appeals Permits NYU Expansion Plan": https://www.citylandnyc.org/new-york-state-court-of-appeals-permits-nyu-expansion-plan/
  - Washington Square News, "Billion-dollar NYU building at 181 Mercer Street nears completion" (August 2022): https://nyunews.com/news/2022/08/10/181-mercer-street-to-open-spring-2023
  - Campus of New York University (faculty opposition, the 2014 ruling): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campus_of_New_York_University
  - Vermont Law Review, "Matter of Glick v. Harvey: A Blight on New York City Community Gardens": https://lawreview.vermontlaw.edu/matter-of-glick-v-harvey-a-blight-on-new-york-city-community-gardens/

### 21. May 1970: the strike and the Courant computer

- **When:** May 1970, days after the US invasion of Cambodia and the killing of four students by the National Guard at Kent State University on 4 May.
- **Where:** Warren Weaver Hall, 251 Mercer Street, home of the Courant Institute (inventory 3.10), facing Gould Plaza. The Loeb Student Center (on the site of today's Kimmel Center, inventory 3.2) and Kimball Hall were also occupied.
- **What happened:** NYU students went on strike and occupied several buildings. In Warren Weaver Hall, the strikers took over the second-floor computing centre of the Atomic Energy Commission, whose CDC 6600 supercomputer was valued at $3.5 million and which strikers called "the war machine". They held it to ransom for $100,000, money they intended to use as bail for a member of the Black Panther Party. Courant faculty and staff, many sympathetic to the anti-war cause, negotiated for the release of the computer and the building. After days of occupation the strikers decided to release the computer and hold a press conference denouncing its presence on campus. As the strikers left, a physics student and a history professor lit the fuse of a bomb in the second-floor computing facility; two young mathematics professors smothered the fuse at the last moment. NYU's News Bureau published a report on the three occupations that September.
- **Why it mattered:** It showed how close Cold War science sat to the campus: the AEC funded over half of Courant's research budget and kept its computer in the building. It is NYU's best-documented episode of the national 1970 student strike.
- **Flags:** **Contested** in its meaning (anti-war protest versus attack on research), not in its facts. The people involved are described by role only.
- **Traces in 2126:** Warren Weaver Hall's second-floor machine room, empty, with the raised floor and cable trays of a 1960s computer centre still visible; the second-floor window through which the CDC 6600 was moved into the building in 1965. Strike posters are paper and gone, but a stencilled slogan on concrete could survive under grime.
- **Sources:**
  - Brit Shields, "The 'Courant Hilton': building the mathematical sciences at New York University", *British Journal for the History of Science* 57 (2024), 425 to 446, open access, citing NYU's 23 September 1970 report "The disruptions at Loeb, Courant and Kimball" (NYU Archives, RG 3.0.7) and Frusciano and Pettit, *New York University and the City* (1997): https://doi.org/10.1017/S000708742300105X
  - Courant Institute news item on the May 1970 takeover: https://cims.nyu.edu/dynamic/news/1194/

### 23. Take Back NYU occupies the Kimmel Center

- **When:** From shortly before 10 pm on 18 February 2009 to 20 February 2009.
- **Where:** The third-floor cafeteria of the Kimmel Center for University Life, 60 Washington Square South (inventory 3.2).
- **What happened:** About 70 students from the group Take Back NYU barricaded themselves into the third-floor cafeteria with tables and chairs. The group had first put three demands to the administration in September 2008 (disclosure of the budget, disclosure of the endowment, and a student seat on the Board of Trustees) and had added more since, including an endowment oversight body of students, faculty and staff. The university let them stay the first night with access to restrooms but said they could not stay longer. Supporters rallied outside; some pushed past officers to join. On 20 February the occupiers were removed. Eighteen were suspended and faced disciplinary review; supporters said these were the negotiators and that the administration had signalled it would talk before refusing. None of the stated demands was met, and the group faded afterwards.
- **Why it mattered:** An early round of demands for financial transparency at NYU that recur in later campaigns. It happened in the building that had replaced the Loeb Student Center, itself occupied in 1970.
- **Flags:** **Contested** (the university's handling versus the students' tactics). No private individuals named.
- **Traces in 2126:** The Kimmel Center's third floor overlooks the park through tall glass: in 2126, a cafeteria with chairs stacked against the doors, as if the last people in had barricaded themselves. That image is the event's echo, not a claim that anything survived from 2009.
- **Sources:**
  - Global Nonviolent Action Database (Swarthmore College), "New York University students occupy cafeteria for transparency, 2009": https://nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu/index.php/node/3088
  - Solidarity, "Student Occupation at NYU" (19 February 2009, a sympathetic contemporary account): https://solidarity-us.org/p2060/
